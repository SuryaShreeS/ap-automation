Accounts payable at H.J. Baker

Five kinds of document arrive in one mailbox, and each one has to be handled differently. This is our understanding of that business — the documents, the decisions, the awkward cases, and what Sage ends up holding.

Client H.J. Baker & Bro., LLC — established 1850 Scope Accounts payable, seven operating companies System of record Sage X3

Introduction

What we set out to understand

H.J. Baker moves sulphur — by rail, by truck, by vessel — across seven operating companies. Accounts payable sits at the end of that chain, turning whatever a vendor sends into a posted document in Sage X3 and a payment that goes out on time.

Every payable document arrives the same way: a PDF attached to an email in the shared AP mailbox. A railcar of molten sulphur, a month's office rent, a nine-million-dollar prepayment, a cancellation, a price settlement — all of it lands in the same place, and nothing about the delivery tells you which kind of document you are holding.

That is the heart of the problem. The current tool treats every document identically, which means a person has to open each one and work out what it is before any real work can start. And because everything is reviewed, nothing is reviewed carefully.

So before designing anything, we spent our time on one question: what are the genuinely different processes hiding inside that inbox? The answer is five. They share an arrival route and nothing else — different triggers, different checks, different people, different documents at the end. This paper sets out each one as we understand it, together with the awkward cases your team deals with in practice.

Two principles came out of those conversations and shaped everything that follows. First, sorting and paperwork should be automatic — recognising a document type, matching it, drafting it, numbering it. Second, judgement stays with people — whether a purchase order is warranted, which invoice a credit reverses, and whether to release the money. Those are not delays to be engineered away. They are your controls.

The organisation

Purchase-order invoices

Goods or services that were ordered properly. A purchase order already exists in Sage and the invoice quotes it. This is the majority of volume, and the case that should need the least human attention.

Recognised by
The invoice quotes a purchase order number
Owned by
Processor, then approver
Becomes in Sage
A purchase invoice PIN######
Stops for a person

Edge cases we have accounted for

The invoice beats the goods
Nothing has been receipted yet, so a three-way match is impossible. The invoice is matched against the order alone and clearly marked as a two-way match, rather than being held up waiting for receipts.
Quantity or price does not agree
This becomes an exception for a processor with the variance shown, not an automatic rejection back to the vendor. Most of these are resolved internally.
One invoice covers many bills of lading
Legitimate for some vendors. Those vendors are held on an approved list so the one-bill-of-lading check does not fight them every month.
A monthly summary covers about a thousand receipts
The open receipts for the period are selected as a set and reconciled against the invoice total. Nobody ticks a thousand boxes.
A load list runs past a thousand lines
Handled whole, across however many pages it takes. Line count is not a limit.
The same invoice arrives twice
Refused before it is created, on the combination of vendor, invoice number and amount — which catches genuine resends without blocking legitimate re-invoicing.
Taxes and freight do not add up
If net plus taxes plus freight does not equal the stated total, the document cannot be submitted and the difference is shown.
Canadian and UK invoices
Currency and tax lines follow the company that owns the invoice, so GST, PST and VAT are treated correctly rather than lumped together.
Payment terms on the document differ from the vendor's default
The document wins, because that is what was agreed on this purchase — but it is flagged so AP can see the override.
NPO

Non-PO invoices

An invoice with no purchase order on it. Two completely different situations arrive looking identical, and telling them apart is a judgement call — which is why this is the one type that always stops for a person.

Recognised by
No purchase order quoted on the document
Owned by
Processor decides; the ordering site raises any order
Becomes in Sage
A purchase invoice PIN######
Stops for a person
The PO decision, then approval
We are not going to pretend purchase orders can be created automatically. You told us plainly why: orders are raised at manual order desks, rail and truck lines come in through imports, and quantities and prices are keyed by hand. A tool that invents the next order number produces a document nobody in Sage trusts. So we do the opposite — show the processor the vendor's most recent order for reference, and wait for the real number.

Edge cases we have accounted for

A purchase order is needed but does not exist yet
The invoice waits in a visible "awaiting order" state with the vendor's last order shown for reference, rather than disappearing into a pile on somebody's desk.
The vendor will never have a purchase order
Office rent, equipment, professional services. There is nothing to match against, so the lines are coded from a standing template and the invoice goes straight to approval. Forcing an order here would be paperwork for its own sake.
The order number is mistyped
Checked against your numbering convention and refused if it does not fit, before it reaches Sage.
The order is already on another invoice
Refused, and the invoice already holding that order is named — so the processor can see immediately whether this is a duplicate or the wrong number.
The ordering site raises the order days later
Attach it then. Processing resumes exactly where it stopped; nothing has to be re-keyed.
The scan is poor and the vendor name is unreadable
Held for a person rather than guessed at. A faxed handwritten ticket and a degraded scan both end up in the same place, with the reason stated.
The document is a statement of account, not an invoice
Held for a person. A statement listing several open items is not something to post, and the ambiguity is flagged rather than resolved silently.
PRE

Prepayments

A small number of vendors are paid before they ship. Money leaves against a lump-sum invoice, then individual shipments draw that balance down. This is a cash instrument, not just an invoice, and it needs watching.

Recognised by
The vendor is marked as prepaid
Owned by
Processor and treasury; CFO on the large ones
Becomes in Sage
A payment, then invoices drawing it down
Stops for a person
Approval, usually above a processor's limit

Edge cases we have accounted for

A second lump sum arrives for the same vendor
It tops up the existing position rather than opening a duplicate, and the consumed percentage is recalculated against the new total.
Shipments approach the prepaid amount
Treasury is warned while there is still time to act, not after the balance has gone.
Shipments exceed the prepaid amount
Flagged for review with the overage stated, because the excess is effectively an unsecured payable against a vendor already paid in advance.
A very large invoice arrives from a prepaid vendor
Size alone does not make it a prepayment. Ordinary shipment true-ups from these vendors run into eight figures, so the document has to say it is a prepayment. Getting this wrong would book a phantom prepayment worth millions.
Payment falls due in two working days
Surfaced on arrival rather than discovered late. A prepayment sitting unnoticed in a queue has a real cost with these vendors.
The document does not name its period
The period is inferred from the invoice date so the prepayment still lands against the right month rather than being left unassigned.
A shipment arrives with no open prepayment
Treated as an ordinary invoice — matched, approved and posted — rather than failing because a balance was expected.
CR

Credits

A cancellation. It looks like an invoice, is often titled "cancellation invoice", and carries a negative total. Its whole purpose is to reverse something already booked — so what it points at matters more than what it says.

Recognised by
A negative total, or cancellation wording
Owned by
Processor, then AP review — always
Becomes in Sage
A credit memo PCM######
Stops for a person
The original invoice reference, then release
Why the original invoice number is non-negotiable. Sage builds the credit memo by pulling the lines from the purchase invoice being credited. Without that reference there is nothing to build from. This is the one place where we deliberately made the system refuse to guess and wait for a person instead.

Edge cases we have accounted for

The credit does not say what it reverses
Processing stops and asks. Guessing would credit the wrong invoice, and that error compounds through the settlement chain.
The referenced number is malformed
Checked against the Sage numbering convention before anything is created, so a typo does not become a posted document.
One credit covers several vendors
The credited invoices are identified individually and the totals split per vendor, rather than posting one lump against whichever vendor happens to be on the header.
The replacement invoice has not arrived
The credit parks visibly, waiting. It clears automatically when the replacement comes in, so nobody has to remember it.
The credit is genuinely standalone
A goodwill adjustment with no replacement to come still requires an AP release. Nothing about a credit is automatic.
A cancellation arrives from a settlement vendor
Treated as a credit first — because the negative total is unambiguous — and its re-invoice is picked up separately as a rebill.
RB

Rebills

Some vendors price provisionally and settle later — at month end, in the middle of the following month, or retroactively across as much as three months. Settlement arrives as a credit reversing the provisional invoice, plus a fresh invoice at the real price.

Recognised by
A settlement vendor re-invoicing at settled price
Owned by
Processor and approver; AP where a period is closed
Becomes in Sage
A purchase invoice linked to the credit and the original
Stops for a person
Approval, and how to post a closed period
What matters here is the audit trail. A settlement leaves three documents behind: the original provisional invoice, the credit that reversed it, and the rebill at the settled price. Six months later somebody will ask why a vendor appears to have been paid twice for one shipment. The answer needs to be one click, not an afternoon.

Edge cases we have accounted for

Settlement reaches back into a closed period
The platform does not decide this. It routes the document to AP with the closed-period condition stated, and waits.
Settlement arrives in pieces across several months
The chain stays open and shows what is still outstanding, rather than appearing complete after the first piece lands.
The rebill arrives before the credit
The chain shows the gap explicitly, so it is obvious a credit is expected rather than the rebill looking like an unrelated new invoice.
The settled price is lower than the provisional
The rebill is smaller than the original and the net effect is visible on the chain, so the vendor position reads correctly at a glance.
Somebody asks about it six months later
The original, the credit and the rebill are one linked chain with all three Sage numbers on it. That is the answer to the audit question before it is asked.

Two rules sit behind that table. Anything an administrator codes routes to somebody else for approval, and nobody approves an invoice they created or edited themselves. Rejecting an invoice requires a written reason, because the person receiving it back needs to know what to fix.

Every field change, match, approval and release is recorded with who did it and when, and cannot be altered afterwards.

Over to you

What we still need from the business

These are decisions only your team can make. None of them block a demonstration; all of them shape the live configuration.

  1. The full AP review rule set. We have built terms mismatches, anything over twenty-five thousand, and all credits. We expect there are more.
  2. The flagged-vendor list — which vendors warrant extra review, and for how many invoices before they are trusted.
  3. The complete prepaid vendor list. Two are configured. Are there others, and who maintains the list?
  4. Settlement vendors and their cadences — who settles at month end, who in the following month, who retroactively.
  5. Closed-period policy for settlements — who decides, and what the cut-off is.
  6. Automatic approval. Deliberately switched off. Small, high-confidence, well-behaved invoices could skip approval entirely — but only once you are satisfied the accuracy has earned it, and probably one company at a time.
